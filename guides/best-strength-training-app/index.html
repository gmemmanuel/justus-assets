Guide · Best strength training app

The best strength training app, depending on what you actually want.

There's no single "best" strength training app. The category splits into coaches (that build your plan), loggers (that record what you did), and algorithmic apps (that pick exercises for you). Each is best at its own job. Here's an honest guide to the category in 2026.

Quick answer

  • For a multimodal coach where strength is part of a bigger plan: Justus.
  • For algorithmic strength selection (the app picks your next workout): Fitbod.
  • For serious strength coaching with an AI + human-upgrade path: Caliber.
  • For minimalist manual logging with no community: Strong.
  • For a logger with optional community: Hevy.
  • For beginner barbell training with a simple proven program: StrongLifts 5x5.
  • The honest framing: "best" depends on whether you want a coach, an algorithm, or a notepad. Pick the category first; pick the product second.

Justus

Best multimodal coach with strength integrated

What it is: A private AI fitness coach that programs strength alongside running, swimming, mobility, and sport in one coordinated plan. Persistent memory across years, conversational adaptation, no social features.

Why it's here: for hybrid athletes and generalists who want the strength plan to coordinate with the rest of their training. The lift you did yesterday actually shapes today's run, and vice versa.

Fitbod

Best algorithmic strength app

What it is: An algorithmic strength training app that picks your next workout based on what you've done. Strong progression logic, exercise demonstrations, smart substitutions.

Why it's here: for lifters who want the app to decide what they're doing today. The algorithm is genuinely good, the UX is purpose-built for the gym, and progression respects fatigue.

Best for: people who only lift and want a sharp algorithm running the show. Around $12.99/month.

Caliber

Best AI + human strength coaching

What it is: A strength-and-conditioning coaching app with a free AI tier and a premium 1-on-1 human-coach tier. Real programming, real check-ins, real nutrition support at the premium level.

Why it's here: for serious lifters who want real coaching and the option of a human in the loop. Depth on strength is real; the human-coach upgrade path is a meaningful product feature.

Best for: lifters focused on strength as the primary goal. AI tier is free; premium human coaching runs into the hundreds per month.

Strong

Best minimalist logger

What it is: A clean, opinionated strength logger with no community, no algorithm, and no AI. Records what you did; gets out of the way.

Why it's here: for experienced lifters who already know what they want to do and just need to log it. The design philosophy is restraint, and it's executed well.

Best for: lifters who write their own programs or follow a fixed one. Free tier; ~$4.99/month Pro.

Hevy

Best logger with optional community

What it is: A polished strength logger with an optional social layer. Routine builder, exercise library, progression charts, and a feed where users share workouts.

Why it's here: for lifters who want a clean logger with the option of a community attached. The social layer is real, but you can mostly ignore it if you want.

Best for: lifters who like sharing workouts with friends. Free tier; ~$4.99–$6.99/month Pro.

StrongLifts 5x5

Best beginner barbell program

What it is: A simple linear-progression barbell program. Two alternating workouts, three days a week, five sets of five reps. Add weight every session until you stall.

Why it's here: for absolute beginners with gym access. The program is famously simple, the progression is conservative, and beginners build a strength base on the major lifts.

Best for: brand-new barbell trainees who want a proven program without options. Free or low-cost.

The three categories within strength apps

Coaches. Build and maintain your plan, adapt when life happens, hold a profile of you. Caliber and Justus live here. They're the heaviest products and the most full-service.

Algorithmic apps. An algorithm picks your next workout from what you've done. Fitbod is the category leader. You're not really being coached — you're being algorithmically programmed.

Loggers. Record what you did. Don't try to coach you. Strong and Hevy are the cleanest examples. Useful if you already know what you want to do.

The category mistake people make is comparing across categories. A logger isn't competing with a coach; it's a different product.

How to pick, in three questions

1. Do you know what you want to do? If yes — you have a program you trust, or you write your own — a logger is enough. Strong or Hevy.

2. If you don't, do you want an algorithm or a coach? An algorithm is faster to onboard and less work to use. A coach holds the long arc and adapts. Fitbod is the algorithmic answer; Caliber or Justus is the coach answer.

3. Do you train only strength, or do you train across modalities? If strength is the whole sport, Fitbod or Caliber. If you also run, swim, do mobility, or play a sport, Justus is the only one of these that programs across all of it.

The hybrid athlete problem

Strength apps are mostly built for people who only lift. Running apps are mostly built for people who only run. The increasing number of trainees who do both — lifting two or three times a week AND running two or three times a week AND adding mobility — are underserved by the single-sport apps that dominate the category.

The interference is real. Heavy lower-body work trashes your legs for tomorrow's run. A long run leaves you systemically fatigued for the next lift. Most strength apps can't see your runs; most running apps treat strength as supplementary. Justus is one of the few products designed to coordinate both.

What "best" actually means

"Best strength app" is a query with no single answer. A reviewer picking "best" is usually picking from a category they care about — sometimes the algorithmic category, sometimes the coaching category, sometimes the logger category. The framing matters.

This guide ranks within each category rather than across. Pick the category first, then pick the product. The "best logger" and the "best coach" aren't competing for the same job.

Common questions about strength training apps.

What's the best strength training app overall?

There isn't one. The category splits into coaches (that build your plan), loggers (that record what you did), and algorithmic apps (that pick exercises for you).

For algorithmic strength selection, Fitbod is the category standard. For minimalist logging, Strong or Hevy. For serious strength coaching, Caliber. For a multimodal coach where strength is part of a bigger plan, Justus. The right pick depends on what you want the app to do.

What's the best strength app for beginners?

For brand-new lifters who want a simple proven program, StrongLifts 5x5 is the classic. For beginners who want the app to pick exercises and progress them, Fitbod is the easy entry point.

For beginners who want a coach to plan their whole training (not just strength), Justus removes the "pick a program" decision entirely — the first interaction is a conversation.

What's the best strength app for hybrid athletes?

Hybrid athletes — people who lift seriously and also do cardio or other modalities — are underserved by single-sport strength apps. Most strength loggers don't reason about your runs, and most running apps treat strength as supplementary. The category is small.

Justus is one of the few products built to program strength alongside running, swimming, and mobility in one coordinated plan.

What's the best free strength training app?

Strong has a free tier that's usable for casual lifters. Hevy has a free tier that's more generous. Caliber's AI-coaching tier is free.

Each has paid upgrades. For a fully featured coach experience, none of the free tiers replicate what a paid coach product (AI or human) does.

What's the best strength app with no social features?

Strong is the cleanest minimalist option — no community by design. Fitbod has no real social layer. Justus has zero social features by architectural design, not just by setting.

Hevy has an optional social layer that you can mostly ignore, but it exists. If avoiding social is a hard requirement, the privacy-by-design products are the cleaner picks.
